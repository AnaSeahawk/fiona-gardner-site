Exploring Water

Water enters us, arises within us, and moves through us. What does it reveal about how we experience reality?

I am exploring water not simply as something we drink, but as a living medium connecting the body, the atmosphere, and the wider natural world. Water enters us, is created within us through our own biology, and moves through us again. I am curious whether our relationship with it shapes how we experience time, space, and reality itself.

Through the microscope, I compare dew with urine: water gathered from the atmosphere and water that has passed through the body. I look at the patterns, colours, and forms they share, especially their blue and green qualities and radiating structures. I draw on philosophy, alchemy, and historical ideas such as those of Wilhelm Reich, while keeping what I observe separate from how I interpret it. Rather than starting with an answer, I want to stay close to the question and let careful observation show where it leads.

Water, the Cosmic Body, and the Unseen Becoming Visible

I have been contemplating whether we truly understand what water is, or whether naming its many forms has led us to overlook a deeper relationship between them. Blood, plasma, urine, lymph, the fluid within our cells, dew on a blade of grass: each has its own name and role, yet water runs through them all. Perhaps the question is not simply what water is, but what it expresses and what it might reveal about the greater whole of which we are a part.

This leads me to the idea of the Cosmic Body: the possibility that the universe is not merely a vast space containing separate things, but a greater whole within which every body, fluid, and form exists. Ancient ideas such as the macrocosm and microcosm, prima materia, anima mundi, and the ether offer language for contemplating it. I cannot claim to have established this; it is a philosophical question I feel drawn to explore. Water may not be the Cosmic Body itself, but it may be one of the ways the Cosmic Body expresses itself in the physical world.

What interests me most is the way the unseen becomes visible: a droplet forms, a boundary appears, a pattern develops.

Within a single droplet, the unseen gathers itself into form, and I am left wondering what else is quietly taking shape just beyond my sight. The microscope becomes a meeting point between observation and contemplation. The water is visible. The form is visible. The greater whole remains the question.
